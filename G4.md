## Motivation

Modern LHC analyses are increasingly constrained not by statistics, but by our
ability to explore large theory spaces, propagate correlated systematic
uncertainties, and iterate on analysis design. In practice, the dominant
bottleneck is repeated detector simulation. Any meaningful change in theory
hypothesis, systematic treatment, or reconstruction strategy typically requires
new GEANT4 production, and this does not scale to Run-4 and HL-LHC conditions.

There has been significant effort to address this through machine-learning
surrogates or by making GEANT differentiable via automatic differentiation. In
our experience, neither approach is currently viable at CMS scale. Global
surrogate models struggle to remain accurate and consistent outside narrow
training domains and are difficult to validate in ways that detector experts
trust. Fully differentiable GEANT, while technically possible, incurs slowdowns
of O(10–100×) due to stochastic branching, complex control flow, and memory
requirements, making it impractical for production workflows. Both approaches
focus on making the simulator differentiable, but this is not what physics
inference actually requires.

What we need are gradients of physics objectives—likelihoods, uncertainties,
Fisher information with respect to theory and nuisance parameters. GEANT enters
this problem only through how detector-level observables respond to small
changes in particle-level and detector parameters. This observation motivates a
different abstraction: rather than replacing or differentiating GEANT, we treat
it as a trusted forward oracle and extract from it the minimal differentiable
structure required for inference.


## Approach

The approach taken here separates simulation fidelity from inference
differentiability. Full GEANT simulation remains the gold standard and is used
offline for calibration, validation, and sensitivity extraction. We do not
attempt to approximate the full microscopic shower development, nor do we
backpropagate through GEANT itself. Instead, we identify the low-dimensional set
of detector observables that actually enter the analysis likelihood and study
how these observables respond to controlled variations in physics and detector
parameters.

These responses are encoded as local sensitivity operators—response Jacobians
extracted from reproducible GEANT runs with fixed random seeds and small
parameter perturbations. Once extracted, these operators define compact,
differentiable effective detector models that can be evaluated cheaply at
inference time. Gradients of physics objectives can then be propagated
end-to-end, through reconstruction and analysis, without rerunning GEANT for
every hypothesis or systematic variation.

This allows gradient-based optimization of analyses, systematic treatments, and
theory sensitivity while remaining fully anchored to GEANT and real detector
data. The approach is deliberately incremental and modular: it requires no
invasive changes to GEANT or CMSSW and fits naturally into existing validation
and approval workflows.


## The Third Path: Differentiable Effective Operators

Rather than choosing between surrogate simulation and fully differentiable
GEANT, this project follows a third path based on differentiable effective
operators. GEANT remains the forward "truth engine", run normally and
unmodified, and continues to define the reference detector response. The goal is
not to model every interaction, but to capture how the detector response
relevant to an analysis changes when underlying parameters change.

In practice, this means working with objects such as ∂(detector
observables)/∂(physics or detector parameters): local response Jacobians, Fisher
information matrices, or related sensitivity operators. These objects can be
estimated using ensembles of standard GEANT runs with controlled parameter
variations and are far lower-dimensional than the full shower description. As a
result, they are tractable to compute, validate, and propagate.

Conceptually, this reframes the problem as one of adjoint physics rather than
differentiable simulation. The strategy mirrors long-standing methods in adjoint
computational fluid dynamics, variational data assimilation, and inverse design,
where gradients are propagated through effective response operators rather than
through every microscopic degree of freedom. We do not differentiate through
every GEANT step for the same reason one does not backpropagate through every
molecule of air when optimizing an aircraft wing. Instead, we propagate adjoint
information through calibrated, physics-respecting sensitivity operators,
enabling end-to-end gradients at experiment scale without sacrificing fidelity
or trust.
